# Angle D — The Exclusion. Retail Locked Out of What Actually Grew.

> **Headline candidate**: *Between 2015 and 2024, the median value created by a US company before its IPO grew approximately tenfold. Retail does not participate in that creation. By the time the company arrives at a public listing — if it ever does — the curve has flattened. Retail buys the residue.*

The page's other angles document extraction. This angle documents *exclusion*. Retail is not just paying the spread. Retail is not in the room where the value gets made.

---

## 1 · The IPO drought — the new normal

| Year | US IPOs (operating-company, excluding SPAC) | Source |
|------|---------------------------------------------|--------|
| 1996 | ~675 (the peak year) | Jay Ritter, University of Florida |
| 2015 | ~170 | Ritter |
| 2017 | ~106 | Ritter |
| 2019 | ~159 | Ritter |
| 2020 | ~218 (pre-SPAC peak) | Ritter |
| 2021 | ~311 (SPAC-driven anomaly) | Ritter |
| 2022 | **~70** | Ritter |
| 2023 | ~108 | Ritter |
| 2024 | **~135** | Renaissance Capital, Ritter |

The 1996 number is the canonical "lost peak." US IPO activity has averaged roughly **one-quarter of the 1990s level for two decades**.

Source: Jay Ritter's "Initial Public Offerings: Updated Statistics" — `https://site.warrington.ufl.edu/ritter/ipo-data/` — the canonical reference for this dataset.

**The structural effect**: companies that would have IPO'd at $200M valuation in 1996 now IPO at $20B — if at all. The early growth phase that retail used to buy now happens entirely in private rounds.

## 5 · The accredited-investor wall

Under US securities law (Rule 501 of Regulation D), to participate in most private offerings — including the unicorn rounds above — an individual must be an **accredited investor**:
- Income: $200K individual, $300K joint, two years consecutive, with the same expected next year, *or*
- Net worth: $1M+, excluding primary residence, *or*
- Professional designation: Series 7/65/82 license, *or* be a "knowledgeable employee" of the investing fund.

**Accredited investors as % of US population**: approximately **13–14%** (Government Accountability Office report 2023, citing ~24M accredited households out of ~131M US households).

Source: GAO-23-XXX (Accredited Investor reports) — `[VERIFY]` exact GAO citation. Also SEC Office of the Investor Advocate Annual Reports.

**The remaining 86%+** of US households cannot legally buy into private rounds. They are restricted to the public market — which, as the IPO drought shows, increasingly does not exist for early-stage growth.

The structural reading: a regulatory regime intended to protect small investors from fraud has become the gate by which they are excluded from the largest value-creation pools of the modern economy.

## 6 · Performance disparity — what retail misses

| Asset class | 2014–2024 annualized return | Retail access |
|-------------|----------------------------|---------------|
| S&P 500 (with dividends reinvested) | ~13% | Yes, freely |
| Private equity (Cambridge Associates US PE index) | ~14–17% net of fees | Accredited only |
| Venture capital (Cambridge Associates US VC) | ~15–25% top quartile, ~5% median | Accredited only |
| Top-decile VC funds (Sequoia, Founders, Benchmark) | ~25%+ | Closed |
| Private credit (Cliffwater Direct Lending Index) | ~9–10% | Mostly accredited |
| Hedge funds (HFRI Composite) | ~5–6% | Accredited only |

Sources: Cambridge Associates Private Equity / Venture Capital benchmarks, Cliffwater Direct Lending Index, HFR.

**Top-quartile PE and VC outperform the S&P meaningfully**. The median is closer to a wash; the *access to the top quartile* is the gate. Retail by definition cannot access the top-quartile funds because the top-quartile funds are oversubscribed by sovereign wealth funds, university endowments, and family offices.

## 7 · The Mag 7 concentration paradox

A defender might object: "Retail can buy index funds; therefore retail accesses growth."

The counter:

- S&P 500: top 10 stocks (effectively Mag 7 + a few) = **~37% of total index market cap** as of late 2024.
- That concentration *understates* the issue, because:
  - Apple, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta, NVIDIA, Tesla all IPO'd before 2015. The retail S&P holder gets exposure to their *post*-listing growth, not their pre-listing growth.
  - The pre-IPO growth of these companies (Apple founded 1976 → IPO 1980 at ~$1.8B valuation; NVIDIA founded 1993 → IPO 1999 at ~$0.6B; etc.) was the period when value creation was steepest.

The data:
- Microsoft IPO 1986 at $0.5B → 2024 ~$3T. **6,000× pre-to-post-IPO.** Pre-IPO retail had no access.
- Amazon IPO 1997 at $0.4B → 2024 ~$2T. **5,000×.** Same.
- NVIDIA IPO 1999 at $0.6B → 2024 ~$3.4T. **5,700×.** Same.

For a 2024 unicorn launching at $50B that someday IPOs at $500B and then grows to $5T: retail captured the **10× post-IPO leg**, not the **1,000× pre-IPO leg**. Index investing is access to the post-listing curve, not the creation curve.

## 8 · The 401(k) restriction (the gate within the gate)

US retirement accounts (401(k), IRA, traditional pension) have historically restricted alternative-asset access:
- ERISA fiduciary standards make plan sponsors wary of including PE / private credit.
- Most 401(k) menus include only registered mutual funds + index ETFs.

**Recent developments**:
- **2020 DoL Information Letter** under Trump 1.0 first permitted limited PE inclusion in target-date funds within 401(k)s, with strict guardrails.
- **August 2024 Trump executive order** (early in the second term) — broadened 401(k) access to alternatives, including PE, private credit, possibly crypto.
- Industry response cautious; broad adoption pending fiduciary clarifications.

The structural reading: retail's *retirement assets* — the largest pool of capital retail controls — have been gated from alternatives for forty years. The gate is now cracking. Whether that helps retail or simply opens a new feeder-channel for fee extraction is the question.

Source: DoL Information Letter Aug 2020, Aug 2024 Executive Order coverage in WSJ / Bloomberg / Pensions & Investments.

## 9 · Crypto VC — the same shape, faster

The 2017–2024 crypto cycle reproduced traditional finance's exclusion pattern in fast-forward:

| Token | Seed/Series A valuation | First exchange listing | Multiple |
|-------|-------------------------|------------------------|----------|
| Solana | ~$0.04 seed (2018) | ~$0.95 listing (2020) | ~24× |
| Solana | $0.04 → $250+ peak | | ~6000× peak |
| Avalanche | $0.50 seed (2020) | $5+ listing | ~10× |
| Aptos | private rounds ~$0.50 | $9+ listing | ~18× |
| EigenLayer | Sequoia / a16z early rounds | EIGEN listing | ~10-20× pre-listing leg |

**Pattern**: insiders (paradigm, a16z, pantera, jump, multicoin) buy at seed/Series A. Retail gets access at the public sale or DEX listing — typically at 10-50× the seed price. The "free market" then drains retail to refund the insiders' allocations.

Sources: TokenTerminal, CryptoRank, Messari, public a16z portfolio disclosures.

## 11 · How this angle would frame the page

If the article uses **exclusion** as its spine:

- **H1**: *Markets in 2015 were boring. That was the point. Retail could buy the early growth of the companies that mattered.*
- **Lead**: the structural change. Companies stay private. Private-market AUM tripled. Public listings dropped or stagnated. Accredited investor wall didn't move.
- **Body**: the IPO drought, the unicorn ladder, the accredited gate, the Mag 7 concentration, the 401(k) restriction, the crypto VC pattern.
- **Closer**: what GM restores. **Retail cannot enter SpaceX. Retail cannot enter Anthropic. But retail can enter the markets GM makes — without an accreditation gate, without an oversubscribed allocation list, without a 10-year wait.**

The page becomes more about *access* than *fairness*. The Cioran register fits: "*The market did not become hostile to retail. The market simply moved into rooms retail is not allowed to enter.*"
