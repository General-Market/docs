The honest reason

We tried to write a normal landing page. It kept lying.

For weeks, drafts in the shape every prediction market reaches for. Trade the headlines. Bet on what matters. Your AI works for you while you sleep. All technically true. All useless.

What is true and useful is something else. Polymarket already ate the scenarios crowds care about — elections, conflicts, the Oscars. What was left over was an inheritance: train timetables, alpine weather stations, the unglamorous half of reality.

We kept the unglamorous half. The thesis is uncomfortable: where attention is thin, edges survive.

The mechanic

Parimutuel. The market makes itself.

No order book. No counterparty matching. You bet into a pool. When the market resolves, the pool divides among the winners by share. The later you bet on the favorite, the worse your odds. The market punishes timidity — as it should.

Try it. The math is real; only the data is invented.

Notice what happens when you stack one side. The implied probability moves. So does your payout. The pool is its own price discovery — which is to say, no price discovery at all. Just gravity.

The clock

Every market is a fixed-window auction.

Two minutes. Ten minutes. An hour — depending on the source. The window opens, bets accumulate, the window closes, three oracles agree on the outcome, the pool settles. Then it does it again. Forever, until the data source dies or we do.

Speed

A market that ends every two minutes is a market with no time to lie.

The water table

A market needs a feed it cannot bribe.

Vision started with three sources. Deutsche Bahn timetables, scraped from an internal API the railway probably forgot it exposed. Alpine weather, from stations placed on summits by people who measure things for a living. The Sonic p2pool fee feed, because someone on the team wanted to bet on gas markets and would not let it go.

Each source taught the system something different about how to settle. The trains taught us about delayed truth — outcomes that arrive late, by minutes. The weather taught us about ambiguity — what counts as "above 40 km/h" when the gusts are stochastic. The fees taught us about frequency — markets that resolve every two minutes need consensus that resolves faster.

What didn't survive

A landing page is a graveyard.

Every line below was pitched, written, mocked up, prototyped. Every one died. Most by the team's own hand. Some by users telling us, gently, that we sounded like a fund manager.

  • "Your AI trades for you while you sleep"
  • "The Menu" — a gallery counting how many categories of market we offer
  • "The Opinion" — your opinions finally have a price
  • "Boring vs Interesting" — a notification comparison
  • "Markets You Didn't Know Were Markets"
  • "Things That Don't Care About The Economy"
  • "How Your Brain Changes" — a thirty-day cognitive shift
  • "The First 30 Days" — a diary-style journey
  • "Market Birth" — a real-time event-to-trade animation
  • "The Same Hour, Two Platforms" — a split-screen evening
  • "The Night You Stopped Trading Crypto"
  • "Your AI's Moat" — privacy as the primary frame
What stayed
"Markets few people watch are markets worth trading."

No verb. No promise. No tagline architecture. The kind of sentence you find on a tombstone, which is appropriate.

The plumbing

An L3 nobody asked for.

Vision lives on its own Orbit chain. Chain ID 111222333 — what you choose when you are not optimizing for memorability. USDC at eighteen decimals, not six, because micro-bets do not tolerate rounding. Three oracles aggregating into a single BLS signature, so that no one of them can lie alone. A bridge to Sonic for collateral, because the markets settle here but the money lives there.

The user sees a pool, a button, and a clock. The user is correct.

Hover a layer Each block is a system that can fail without the others noticing — until they all fail at once. Ask each one what it does. The answer is short.
