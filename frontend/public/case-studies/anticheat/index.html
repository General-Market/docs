CASE STUDY № 002 · GENERAL MARKET

The Anti-Cheat.

Trading has eight named exploits. Video games solved all of them between 1992 and 2020. We did the embarrassing thing — we read the literature.

11 min read By the General Market team 2026

Markets are the largest competitive multiplayer game on earth. They have never had an anti-cheat.

For three decades, video games and financial markets faced an identical question: how do you let two adversaries play a fair game over a network where one of them is lying?

Games answered the question. Markets answered around it.

The cost of that asymmetry has a number, and the number is large.

$1.5Bsince 2020
Extracted from Ethereum users by MEV bots — front-running, sandwich attacks, liquidation racing. The mempool is public. The bot reads. The bot wins.
$3.8Bper year
Paid to retail brokers by market makers in exchange for routing your order. You think the broker works for you. The broker works for whoever bid for your flow.
$920Msingle fine
JPMorgan's 2020 settlement for spoofing precious metals and treasuries. Place orders. Cancel before they fill. Move the price. Profit. The bank did this for eight years.
$1Tin 36 minutes
Market cap evaporated during the 2010 Flash Crash, attributed in part to a single trader spoofing E-mini futures from a London bedroom. He kept doing it for five more years before anyone caught him.
$50B+2024 alone
Crypto liquidations, most cascading off coordinated stop-hunting on thinly-protected venues. The hunter sees the stops. The stops cannot see the hunter.
$25Bindustry
Annual revenue of high-frequency trading firms whose entire edge is microsecond co-location. They are not smarter. They are closer to the wire. The race ends one foot from the matching engine.

These numbers are the visible cost. The invisible cost is bigger: the retail trader who lost the trade and never knew why. The market maker who quit because the toxic flow was structural. The protocol that died because the bots got there first. The trust that never accumulated because the game was rigged in ways the player could not see.

Regulators have been writing rules against this since the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. Ninety-two years. The exploits multiplied. The names changed. The mechanism never did.

"You cannot fix a competitive game by writing more rules. You fix it by changing the rules of the game itself."

Video games figured this out by 1998.

They had to. A multiplayer game with cheaters has no players. Six months of unchecked aimbots and the lobby is empty. The economics of gaming made anti-cheat existential. The economics of trading made it optional.

So gaming built the anti-cheat. Six architectural primitives, between 1992 and 2020. Each one solved a class of cheating. Together they made competitive online gaming possible.

We took those six primitives and pointed them at trading.

02The six primitives

Each one was invented to solve a specific class of cheating in games. Each one is a complete solution to a specific class of exploit in trading. They were built decades apart, by people who never spoke to each other. Read in order, they describe the architecture of any fair competitive system.

01

Fog of War

DUNE II·WESTWOOD STUDIOS·1992
In gaming

Real-time strategy games face a structural problem: the server simulates a map, but each player is supposed to see only their own corner of it. If you can see your opponent's base, you know what they are building, and the strategic guessing game collapses into mechanical execution.

Westwood Studios solved it by refusing to send the data. The server holds the full map. Each client receives only the cells visible to that player's units. Areas outside vision are not "hidden" in the UI — they are not transmitted at all. A "maphack" cheat that reads the client's memory finds nothing because there is nothing there to find.

Every RTS since has shipped this. Every FPS server-side fog system descends from it. It is the difference between obscuring information and refusing to leak it.

In trading

Public mempools and order books are the opposite architecture. The order is broadcast, hashed, gossiped, watched, and front-run before it reaches the matching engine. Every front-running bot is a maphack on a system that put its map in the address space of every player.

General Market's blocks apply Westwood's rule. The order is committed as a cryptographic hash during the submit phase. The plaintext bet is never on the wire. Other traders, MEV bots, and the protocol itself read the hash and find an opaque commitment. The reveal phase publishes the plaintext only after the submission window has closed and the price is no longer movable. Wallhacks read memory. The memory contains nothing.

02

Server-Authoritative Resolution

QUAKE·id SOFTWARE / CARMACK·1996
In gaming

Early multiplayer FPS games trusted the client. The client said "I shot the enemy" and the server believed it. Players cheated in obvious ways: aimbots claimed perfect hits, speedhacks claimed impossible movement, packet editors claimed kills that never happened. The server had no recourse.

John Carmack's Quake architecture inverted the trust direction. The server became the source of truth. The client was reduced to a viewer that submitted inputs and rendered the response. The client could lie about what it intended; it could not lie about what occurred. If the client claimed a hit but the server simulation showed a miss, the miss was the truth. Counter-Strike 2's "sub-tick" architecture is the latest descendant of this — even fractional-frame inputs are validated server-side.

This single architectural decision killed an entire generation of cheats and made every modern competitive game possible.

In trading

Trading has the equivalent of a client-trusted FPS. Wallets sign messages claiming to have sent transactions. Brokers claim to have routed orders fairly. Exchanges claim to have matched at the best price. The participants police themselves under regulatory paperwork that arrives years after the harm.

On General Market, the chain is the server and the trader is the client. The trader proposes a bet. The chain decides whether it was submitted in the window, sealed correctly, committed before the cutoff, and resolved against the consensus oracle's price. The trader cannot lie about any of this — not because the protocol catches the lie afterward, but because the lie is structurally impossible to express. The oracle settles the price. The client proposes. The server disposes.

03

Lockstep Simulation

STARCRAFT·BLIZZARD ENTERTAINMENT·1998
In gaming

StarCraft tournaments have decided millions of dollars over thirty years. The game ran over 56k modems. Every keystroke from every player had to feel simultaneous on every screen. Naively, this is impossible — packets travel at finite speed, latencies vary, the player on the slower connection should always lose.

Blizzard's answer was lockstep. The game proceeds in fixed ticks. For tick N to advance, every player must submit their input for tick N. The slowest player gates the simulation. When all inputs are received, all of them resolve simultaneously. There is no "first" — the order in which packets arrived inside the tick window is discarded. Every machine in the game runs the identical deterministic simulation, so any divergence means cheating, and cheating becomes a desync the game can detect.

This is what made competitive RTS playable across continents. It is also why StarCraft replays are perfectly reproducible — same inputs, same outcome, on every machine, forever.

In trading

Modern trading is the inverse. Latency is currency. The matching engine processes orders in arrival order, so every microsecond of physical proximity to the engine is dollars. Citadel and Virtu lease floor space inside exchange data centers. The race is not won by being right. It is won by being closer.

A General Market block runs a tick. Every order submitted during the submit phase is collected and resolved simultaneously at reveal. Arriving in the first millisecond of the window is mathematically identical to arriving in the last. Co-location buys nothing. The slow connection and the fiber connection settle on the same line. Latency arbitrage is not policed. It does not exist as a category.

04

Rollback Netcode

GGPO·TONY CANNON·2006
In gaming

Fighting games are the cruelest test of fair networking. The combos are frame-perfect. A two-frame delay can mean the difference between champion and bystander. The first generation of online fighting games used "delay-based" netcode — every player ate input lag equal to the round-trip time. Nobody could play. The genre nearly died on the move from arcades to the internet.

Tony Cannon's GGPO library, released in 2006, replaced delay with prediction. Each client predicts the opponent's next input, simulates forward, and rolls back the simulation if the prediction was wrong when the real input arrives. The local game feels lag-free. The network catches up invisibly.

The crucial constraint: once an input is committed for a frame, it cannot be retracted. Not on lag. Not on a crash. Not on a regret. The input is the input, and the simulation will accept it whether or not the player still wants it. Every modern fighting game (Street Fighter 6, Guilty Gear Strive, Tekken 8, Smash) ships rollback. Without it, the genre would not have an esports scene.

In trading

Spoofing is the trading-world equivalent of input retraction. Place a large buy order. Watch the market react. Cancel the order before it fills. Place the real, opposite order at the better price. JPMorgan paid $920 million for doing this. Navinder Sarao crashed the S&P 500 from his parents' house doing this. The exploit lives in the gap between "expressing intent" and "committing to it."

General Market closes the gap. The submit phase accepts a sealed bet. The reveal phase opens it. Once revealed, the bet cannot be cancelled, modified, retracted, or apologized for. There is no order-pull, no last-look, no rage-cancel. The trader who placed the bet is the trader who took the risk. Spoofing is a feature of order-book architectures. Blocks do not have one.

05

Memory-Encrypted Client State

EAC / BATTLEYE·2014
In gaming

Modern competitive shooters cannot rely on fog of war alone. Some game state has to live on the client — sound positions, animation skeletons, hit volumes. A determined cheat reads that memory and reconstructs enemy positions through walls.

Easy Anti-Cheat and BattlEye, the two dominant kernel-level anti-cheats, encrypt sensitive client memory at runtime. The data is in RAM, but it is illegible without keys the cheat does not have. The wallhack reads the memory and finds entropy. To defeat the encryption, the cheat would have to compromise the kernel itself — and at that point the operating system has bigger problems than your game.

Used by: Fortnite, Apex Legends, Rust, Tarkov, Elden Ring, every game where competitive integrity is a business requirement.

In trading

Sometimes the trade has to be visible to someone before settlement. The exchange has to see it. The clearing house has to see it. The brokerage has to see it. Each of these "trusted" intermediaries is a memory-leak surface. Information sold to HFTs as "order flow." Front-runs by employees with privileged access. Insider tip-offs from inside the trusted layer.

The block's commitment hash is the trading-world equivalent of memory encryption. The bet is "in the system" — committed, signed, on chain — but its plaintext is not legible to anyone, including General Market itself, until the reveal phase. We cannot front-run a bet we cannot read. Neither can the chain. Neither can the validator. The trade exists in a sealed envelope until the envelope is opened, and the envelope can only be opened on schedule.

06

Behavioral Statistical Detection

FACEIT ANTI-CHEAT·2016
In gaming

FACEIT runs the most respected third-party Counter-Strike league. Their anti-cheat is feared because it does not bother trying to catch the cheat itself. It catches the shape of the result.

Honest players exhibit a distribution: their reaction times follow a curve, their tracking smoothness has noise, their headshot rates correlate with rank. A cheater's distribution is different — sub-150ms reactions across thousands of rounds, kill-ratios that no human in the player's bracket has ever produced. FACEIT bans on the statistical signature, even when they cannot identify the exploit. The argument is simple: if no honest player produces this distribution, the player is not honest.

This works because the structure of competitive games makes cheating measurable. The cheat has to express itself somewhere. The structure makes that somewhere visible.

In trading

Insider trading is invisible on a single perp trade. Someone bets long ETH thirty minutes before an ETF approval announcement. Either they got lucky or they got a tip. The SEC investigates for years. Sometimes they win. Often they don't.

General Market makes insider information statistically futile by structure. Each block bundles 100 markets — BTC up, ETH down, SOL up, HYPE down, and 96 others. To "win" the block, the trader has to predict all 100 outcomes correctly. Insider information on one market gives them a 100% edge on that one bet. The other 99 markets are random. The math gives the insider a winrate of 0.1%.

This is the FACEIT principle, parameterized: we can scale the bundle. Want it harder? Add more markets per block. The insider's edge on a 100-market block is 1%. On a 1,000-market block, it's 0.1%. On a 10,000-market block, it is statistically indistinguishable from random. The defense has a knob, and we control the knob.

07

Kernel-Level Pre-Game Integrity

RIOT VANGUARD · VALORANT·2020
In gaming

Every anti-cheat before Vanguard had the same fundamental flaw: it loaded after the operating system, sometimes after the cheat itself. A determined cheater could install a rootkit at boot, the rootkit would load before the anti-cheat, and from that privileged position it could lie to every check the anti-cheat ran.

Riot Games' answer was Vanguard. It loads as a kernel driver at system boot. It is running before any user-space process exists, before any cheat can load, before the game has even started. From the kernel, it watches every signed driver, every memory access, every system call. The verifier is structurally below the player.

Players hated it. The cheaters hated it more. Valorant has the lowest cheater rate of any modern shooter. The trade is consensual: a fair game requires a verifier that the player cannot reach.

In trading

Trading has the inverse architecture. The trader is the kernel. They sign their own transactions, choose their own counterparties, audit their own behavior. The "verifier" is a regulator who arrives years later with a subpoena. By the time the verification runs, the exploit has finished, the money has moved, and the harm is permanent.

The chain is General Market's Vanguard. It runs before every trade, sees every order in its committed form, sequences them in deterministic batches, and settles them under oracle consensus. The trader cannot reach below it. They can submit, reveal, and accept the outcome. They cannot reorder, censor, or front-run, because the layer that does the reordering is structurally inaccessible to them. The verifier sits below the player. Riot got there in 2020. We got there because they did.

03What disappears

Apply the six primitives to a market and the eight named exploits do not get harder. They become structurally absent. There is no spoofing because there is no order to retract. There is no front-running because there is no plaintext to read. There is no latency arbitrage because the tick erases time within the window.

The cheats are not punished. They have nothing to do.

The knife.

Counter-Strike has been playable since 1999. Street Fighter since 1991. StarCraft tournaments have decided millions of dollars for three decades.

None of these systems leak inputs. None allow front-running. None allow rage-cancel. The engineers who built them were clear about the rules of their craft: a competitive game where the server can be fooled is not a game.

Trading was never engineered with that constraint. It was patched, regulated, lobbied, and litigated. The exploits stayed because the exploits paid the lobbyists. Ninety-two years of rule-writing produced a market where retail orders are sold by the broker who collects them, where bots see your trade before you do, where the same firm that fines you for spoofing employs the spoofers.

We did not invent any of the answers. The answers existed. We did the embarrassing thing.

We read the literature.

Counter-Strike beat MEV in 1999. We are twenty-seven years late.
