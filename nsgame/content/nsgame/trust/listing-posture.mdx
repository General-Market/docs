A protocol names real performers and runs short-window markets on the public count of their views. The first question every lawyer, every journalist, every regulator brings to this page is the same: *how is that allowed.* The honest answer is that the law has not decided.

What follows is three observations and one workflow. The observations describe a posture the law currently tolerates. The workflow describes what happens when a subject objects. Read them in order.

## The protocol is not the publisher

The protocol is a Solana program. It accepts transactions from any wallet. It is permissionless. It does not know who minted a market, who funded it, or whether the named subject ever heard of it. nsgame.org is one frontend pointed at that program — a curated subset, a window. Other frontends can exist. The website is something we control. The protocol is something we don't.

This is the same posture every neutral-tool platform takes: the chain that hosts the program, the wallet that signs the transaction, the messaging layer that ferries the call. Section 230 in the United States, the e-Commerce Directive in the European Union, the platform-immunity carve-outs in most other jurisdictions — they exist because lawmakers concluded, decades ago, that the rails should not be liable for what travels on them.

There is a limit. Permissionless deploy platforms historically delist when credible objections arrive. The protocol holds; the website honours the documented takedown workflow.

## The takedown workflow is the operational answer

A subject — or a verified representative — can use the [Subject Removal Policy](/trust/subject-removal-policy) form to delist a market from nsgame.org. The on-chain market remains; the website's catalog removes the listing across rounds and across redesigns. The form is the route. The workflow handles individual subject objections, regulator notices, and court orders through the same channel, with different SLAs.

The relationship between named subjects and the protocol is documented. The takedown workflow is the route to changing it. The website does not pre-clear every market against every subject's right of publicity; it responds to every credible objection that arrives.

## The law has not caught up

A market named "ALICE vs BOB" wagering on whose view-count grows faster is — arguably — not a use of likeness to sell a product in the traditional right-of-publicity sense. It is wagering on a public metric. Courts have not ruled on this for prediction markets. The pump.fun-era memecoin cases mostly tested fraud and unregistered-securities theories, not publicity rights. Right-of-publicity statutes vary by state and country and were written before any of this existed. The law has not decided.

It will. The first major judgment in this area will set precedent that may retroactively reshape a class of markets — perhaps including ours, perhaps including most of them. The documented takedown workflow is the operational answer; the rest is what the courts will decide. We document our current posture and update it when the law moves.

## Where the analogy fails

We have leaned, in the sections above, on the pump.fun parallel. Honesty requires that we name the places it does not extend. The protocol-versus-frontend separation maps onto our case. The rest does not, or does only with strain. Eight failures, each followed by the operational position.

**1. Face vs. number.** Pump.fun makes money on trading fees from a market the user voluntarily enters with crypto they already hold; the performer is not the product. Right-of-publicity law tests whether identity was used commercially to sell something. Aggregators map onto that test perfectly; memecoins map onto it awkwardly. The First Amendment covers the porn itself. It also covers, under *Bartnicki v. Vopper* and *Florida Star v. B.J.F.*, lawfully-obtained truthful information on matters of public concern. The website does not display likeness — it displays the handle the subject chose and the counter the platform publishes. Right-of-publicity attaches to identity-as-merchandise; the wager is on a delta. The transformative-use test from *Comedy III v. Saderup* asks whether a use adds expression beyond reproduction; converting an attention curve into a probabilistic instrument is, plausibly, that. We name it as a hypothesis, not a defence.

**2. Volume as camouflage.** Pump.fun mints tens of thousands of tokens a day. Any individual infringement is a needle in a haystack the platform can plausibly claim it cannot moderate in real time — the same defence YouTube used in *Viacom v. YouTube*. A curated grid of twenty-five performer faces has no such defence. Curation is editorial. Editorial is, under Section 230 and its successors and under the *Force v. Facebook* and *Zeran v. AOL* lines of cases, sometimes protected and sometimes not — selection among third-party submissions has historically been treated as immune. The website performs editorial selection over a small public-metric data source. The DAO will administer takedown governance over that selection at mainnet — see [Decentralization & The DAO](/trust/decentralization-and-dao).

**3. Performers are not a uniform plaintiff class.** Pump.fun's per-token model means an angry performer raises an objection against one ticker. The website-versus-protocol architecture answers this differently: the website is mortal, the protocol is not. The DAO can route a new website. The redundancy is at the protocol layer, not the frontend. The website and the protocol are separable; the protocol persists independent of any single deployment of the website.

**4. DMCA safe harbour does not fit.** Pump.fun can argue user-generated content with a takedown system and no actual knowledge — wobbly, but arguable. A protocol whose entire business model is reading public profiles from a known scraped source has actual knowledge baked into the product definition. Reading a public counter is not the kind of act the DMCA addresses. The website does not invoke DMCA safe harbour because it does not need it. The takedown workflow exists because objection is the only honest answer when a public figure prefers obscurity.

**5. Code-is-speech does not extend to performer directories.** Two decades of EFF litigation — *Bernstein v. US*, the Tornado Cash defence — argue that publishing smart contracts is protected expression. There is no equivalent tradition for "publishing a directory of porn performers is protected expression." The First Amendment covers the porn itself; it does not cover unauthorised commercial use of someone's face to drive traffic. The protocol is speech (a deployer for prediction-market PDAs); the curated frontend is treated as a separate surface with separate protections. We name this distinction rather than letting the reader assume otherwise.

**6. Jurisdictional arbitrage is weaker for a website than for tokens.** A Solana token has no physical presence. The website has a domain, a server, a CDN, an analytics account, an SSL cert, a DNS registrar. Each is a pressure point a plaintiff can squeeze in their home country. The website and the protocol are separable; the protocol persists independent of any single deployment of the website.

**7. The regulatory wind is mixed.** Crypto regulation in 2026 is loosening — new SEC guidance, the current US administration's posture, stablecoin legislation. Adult-content regulation is tightening — age-verification laws in over twenty US states, the UK Online Safety Act, the EU DSA. The blocklist is updated as jurisdictions move. The website is turned off in a jurisdiction before the protocol is asked to lie about geography.

**8. Cultural permission is hostile.** Memecoin chaos is read as a joke; degenerate gambling is the point and everyone is in on it. Adult aggregators are read with suspicion; the surrounding culture (revenge porn, deepfakes, trafficking concerns) creates priors that any new entrant inherits. Judges, juries, and journalists arrive with priors. The product names what it does and what it does not do. Hostility is not conceded as correct.

**The cumulative position.** The pump.fun analogy explains the protocol-versus-frontend separation and nothing else. The product runs with the smallest possible catalog (twenty-five today, curated to liquidity), the cleanest possible takedown workflow, no DMCA shelter, no volume camouflage, no editorial pretence. The takedown workflow remains the route through which any credible objection — individual, regulatory, or judicial — is honoured.

Markets reference public counters indexed by handle; the handle is identification, not the commercial offering. The wager is on the counter. Stripping the names produces abstract numerical contests that do not function as a market — a different product. The names are how the markets are findable. The website honours credible takedown notices through the documented workflow.

## What we do when an objection arrives

The protocol does not delist. The website does. A subject — or a verified representative, or a court order, or a credible non-consensual-content report — files the form documented in [Subject Removal Policy](/trust/subject-removal-policy). The website sends a verification link. Proof of identity is accepted, used once, and discarded. The market is delisted from nsgame.org permanently, across rounds, across redesigns. The chain still remembers; nobody, including the website operator, can change that.

The website does not pre-clear every market against every subject's right of publicity. The cost of doing so on every wallet's mint is unaffordable. The website responds, instead, to every credible objection that arrives. A market without an objection runs. A market with one comes down. A single takedown letter, free to write, costs the website nothing to honour. That is the practical workflow.

## What we are, in law

Three sentences, one frame each. The protocol is speech and a tool — *Bernstein v. US* established that publishing cryptographic source code is protected expression; *Coin Center v. Yellen* and *Van Loon v. Treasury* (5th Cir., Nov 2024) extended the doctrine to non-custodial smart-contract infrastructure. The website is a curated index of third-party submissions — Section 230(c)(1) of the Communications Decency Act and the *Force v. Facebook* line treat selection among third-party content as immune in many configurations. The wager is on a lawfully-obtained public metric — *Bartnicki v. Vopper*, *Florida Star v. B.J.F.*, and the transformative-use test from *Comedy III v. Saderup* describe the speech surface that wraps a public counter and a probabilistic instrument built on top of it.

State right-of-publicity statutes — California Civil Code §3344, New York Civil Rights §50–51, Tennessee's ELVIS Act, the Indiana, Ohio, and Washington analogues — reach name-only commercial use. The posture has not been tested against them. Anti-SLAPP statutes — Cal. Civ. Proc. §425.16, the Texas TCPA, and parallel laws in roughly thirty US jurisdictions — shift fees onto plaintiffs whose suits target speech on matters of public concern. A plaintiff considering suit should know this is part of the geometry.

The defences exist. The takedown workflow remains the primary operational answer.

## What we do for non-consensual content

The Take It Down Act (federal US, signed 19 May 2025) requires takedown of non-consensual intimate imagery within 48 hours of credible report. The website does not host imagery. It does display markets that reference subjects whose underlying content may have been distributed without consent. The distinction is real and does not, by itself, exempt the website.

The SLA: any credible NCII report routes to a fast-track removal — within 48 hours of receipt, the named market is delisted from nsgame.org. The form is the same as the standard request in [Subject Removal Policy](/trust/subject-removal-policy); the priority flag is different. Court orders are not required for NCII reports. Enough information to identify the subject and the market is.

BIPA (740 ILCS 14), the Texas CUBI (Bus. & Com. Code §503.001), and the parallel biometric statutes are not implicated. The protocol processes only handles — strings — and counters — integers. No biometric identifier, no face template, no voice sample, no derived facial geometry. If that ever changes, this paragraph changes first.

A regulator's notice or a court order is honoured at the same speed.

## The contingency

The protocol is named-subject today. The legal posture documented above describes that mode. The protocol also supports a second mode — anonymized markets, where each pair is identified by an abstract market code and no public handle appears in the UI, the URL, or the on-chain event log.

The anonymized mode is not the live product. It is the operational contingency. If a binding judgment, regulation, or court order requires the protocol to operate without named references in a given jurisdiction, that jurisdiction can be served by the anonymized mode without a program redeploy. The wagering mechanic is unchanged: public counters, parimutuel pools, oracle settlement.

The contingency exists so the answer to *"what does the protocol do if a court tells it to stop naming subjects?"* is neither *"shut down"* nor *"ignore the order."* There is a third option, and it has been designed.

## What this is not

This page does not claim a permission slip. No subject named has consented to the market on themselves; consent was not sought because, under the protocol's current posture, it is not legally required for a public-metric wager — a fact about the law's silence, not a blessing from it. The page does not claim the law is settled. It does not claim the reading of right-of-publicity offered here will survive its first judicial test.

The page claims only this. The protocol is a neutral wagering rail. The website is a curated window onto it. The law has not yet ruled and may rule against the posture. When a subject objects through the documented workflow, the website removes the market. The chain keeps its ledger; nobody can change that, including the website operator.

If that arrangement is unacceptable to you, the protocol does not need you, and the document you are reading does not need to convince you.
