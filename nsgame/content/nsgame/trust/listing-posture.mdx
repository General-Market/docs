A protocol names real performers and runs short-window markets on the public count of their views. None of those performers signed a release. None were asked. The first question every lawyer, every journalist, every regulator brings to this page is the same: *how is that allowed.* The second question is the one nobody asks aloud: *and what happens when somebody decides it isn't.*

The answer is not "it's legal." The answer is three observations and one promise. The three observations describe a posture the law currently tolerates. The promise describes what we do when toleration runs out for a particular subject. Read them in order. Reject them as a set or accept them as a set. There is no middle.

## We are not the publisher, on paper

The protocol is a Solana program. It accepts transactions from any wallet. It is permissionless. It does not know who minted a market, who funded it, or whether the named subject ever heard of it. nsgame.org is one frontend pointed at that program — a curated subset, a window. Other frontends can exist. Other frontends will exist. The website is something we control. The protocol is something we don't.

This is the same posture every neutral-tool platform takes: the chain that hosts the program, the wallet that signs the transaction, the messaging layer that ferries the call. Section 230 in the United States, the e-Commerce Directive in the European Union, the platform-immunity carve-outs in most other jurisdictions — they exist because lawmakers concluded, decades ago, that the rails should not be liable for what travels on them. Token launchers operate on this. Memecoin platforms operate on this. So do we.

The honest limit. It works until it doesn't. Pump.fun delists tokens when the lawyer letter arrives — Hailey Welch, Iggy Azalea, Caitlyn Jenner all triggered takedowns once their teams pressed. The long ledger of "pump fun delisted" results is the historical record. What you see on the homepage of any neutral-tool protocol is what survived, not what was launched. nsgame inherits the same posture and the same limit. The protocol holds. The website yields when a credible objection arrives.

## The cost-of-enforcement runs in our favor

A subject's lawyer can send one cease-and-desist and a market disappears from nsgame.org within a day. The on-chain market remains; on-chain it is irrelevant, because nobody finds a market without the frontend. But sending that letter costs the subject real money. Most subjects choose their battles, and a small-pool market is a poor battlefield. The pump.fun-era lawsuits were filed by the few subjects whose memecoins attracted enough volume to be worth the legal bill. The thousand markets beneath that threshold were ignored not because they were permissioned but because they were not worth the lawyer's afternoon.

This is selection bias and silence — neither consent nor opposition. The law treats unobjected-to commentary on public figures differently than it treats commentary they protest; *Cardtoons v. MLBPA* (10th Cir. 1996) treats public-figure silence as evidentiary, not blank. We do not pretend the silence is a signature. We do not pretend it is meaningless either. The performers we list have not consented; they have not, mostly, objected. The economics of enforcement and the economics of obscurity are doing work the law has not yet done. We benefit from a posture we did not buy and would not call endorsement.

## The law has not caught up

A market named "ALICE vs BOB" wagering on whose view-count grows faster is — arguably — not a use of likeness to sell a product in the traditional right-of-publicity sense. It is wagering on a public metric. Courts have not ruled on this for prediction markets. The pump.fun-era memecoin cases mostly tested fraud and unregistered-securities theories, not publicity rights. The SEC has expressed an interest in tokens-tied-to-persons; right-of-publicity statutes vary by state and country and were written before any of this existed. The law has not decided.

It will. The first major judgment in this area will set precedent that retroactively kills a class of markets — perhaps including ours, perhaps including most of them. We do not know which way it goes. Anyone who tells you they do is selling a position, not describing one. We operate in the window before that judgment because the window is open. We will operate differently after it closes. Building anything else on the assumption of regulatory stasis is a category of self-deception we declined.

## Where the analogy fails

We have leaned, in the sections above, on the pump.fun parallel. Honesty requires that we name the places it does not extend. The protocol-versus-frontend separation maps onto our case. The rest does not, or does only with strain. Eight failures, each followed by our actual position.

**1. Face vs. number.** Pump.fun makes money on trading fees from a market the user voluntarily enters with crypto they already hold; the performer is not the product. Right-of-publicity law tests whether identity was used commercially to sell something. Aggregators map onto that test perfectly; memecoins map onto it awkwardly. The First Amendment covers the porn itself. It also covers, under *Bartnicki v. Vopper* and *Florida Star v. B.J.F.*, lawfully-obtained truthful information on matters of public concern. We do not display likeness — we display the handle the subject chose and the counter the platform publishes. Right-of-publicity attaches to identity-as-merchandise; we sell wagers on a delta. The transformative-use test from *Comedy III v. Saderup* asks whether a use adds expression beyond reproduction; converting an attention curve into a probabilistic instrument is, plausibly, that. We name it as a hypothesis, not a defence.

**2. Volume as camouflage.** Pump.fun mints tens of thousands of tokens a day. Any individual infringement is a needle in a haystack the platform can plausibly claim it cannot moderate in real time — the same defence YouTube used in *Viacom v. YouTube* and won. A curated grid of twenty-five performer faces has no such defence. Curation is editorial. Editorial is, under Section 230 and its successors and under the *Force v. Facebook* and *Zeran v. AOL* lines of cases, sometimes protected and sometimes not — selection among third-party submissions has historically been treated as immune. We do not rely on that protection; we behave as if we did not have it. That is a posture, not a confession. Our position: we are not Viacom-v.-YouTube. The website performs editorial selection over a small public-metric data source. The DAO will administer takedown governance over that selection at mainnet — see [Decentralization & The DAO](/trust/decentralization-and-dao). We do not hide behind volume because we do not have it.

**3. Performers are not a uniform plaintiff class.** Pump.fun's per-token model means an angry performer kills one ticker. Our model means she kills the website. The litigious performers are a minority but a guaranteed minority — anyone who has left the industry, found religion, or is rebuilding a brand. Our position: the website is mortal. The protocol is not. The DAO can route a new website. The redundancy is at the protocol layer, not the frontend. We accept that any individual lawsuit may end nsgame.org and not the protocol.

**4. DMCA safe harbour does not fit.** Pump.fun can argue user-generated content with a takedown system and no actual knowledge — wobbly, but arguable. A protocol whose entire business model is reading public profiles from a known scraped source has actual knowledge baked into the product definition. Reading a public counter is not the kind of act the DMCA addresses. We do not invoke DMCA safe harbour because we do not need it. The takedown workflow exists not because we infringe, but because objection is the only honest answer when a public figure prefers obscurity.

**5. Code-is-speech does not extend to performer directories.** Two decades of EFF litigation — *Bernstein v. US*, the Tornado Cash defence — argue that publishing smart contracts is protected expression. There is no equivalent tradition for "publishing a directory of porn performers is protected expression." The First Amendment covers the porn itself; it does not cover unauthorised commercial use of someone's face to drive traffic. Our position: we do not invoke code-is-speech for the website. The protocol is speech (a deployer for prediction-market PDAs); the curated frontend is not. We name this distinction rather than letting the reader assume otherwise.

**6. Jurisdictional arbitrage is weaker for us than for tokens.** A Solana token has no physical presence. We have a domain, a server, a CDN, an analytics account, an SSL cert, a DNS registrar. Each is a pressure point a plaintiff can squeeze in their home country without ever serving us. Cloudflare drops adult sites under pressure routinely; they almost never drop smart contracts. Our position: the website is mortal. We accept it. The protocol is the redundancy.

**7. The regulatory wind blows the wrong way.** Crypto regulation in 2026 is loosening — new SEC guidance, the current US administration's posture, stablecoin legislation. Adult-content regulation is tightening — age-verification laws in over twenty US states, the UK Online Safety Act, the EU DSA. Pump.fun rides a tailwind. We ride into a headwind that strengthens every quarter. Our position: the blocklist will grow. We accept it. We will turn off the website in a jurisdiction before we ask the protocol to lie about geography.

**8. Cultural permission is hostile.** Memecoin chaos is read as a joke; degenerate gambling is the point and everyone is in on it. Adult aggregators are read as predatory; the surrounding culture (revenge porn, deepfakes, trafficking concerns) makes any new entrant look guilty by association. Judges, juries, and journalists arrive with priors. Ours are hostile. Our position: we do not ask to be read as funny. We accept that the cultural read is hostile. We do not concede that hostility is correct. The product names what it does and what it does not do; readers arrive with priors we cannot manage and will not pretend to.

**The cumulative position.** The pump.fun analogy explains the protocol-versus-frontend separation and nothing else. We are weaker than the launchers we cite on every other axis. We do this anyway, with the smallest possible catalog (twenty-five today, curated to liquidity), with the cleanest possible takedown workflow, with no DMCA shelter, no volume camouflage, no editorial pretence. The first big judgment will likely kill a class of products like ours. We position to delist on credible notice and accept the eventual end of the class.

We do this because the alternative is to not name subjects at all — and the moment we strip the names, the markets become abstract numerical contests with no audience, which is a different product nobody asked for. The names are how the markets are findable. The wagers are on the public counters. We do not pretend the names are decorative; we do not concede they are the merchandise. We name knowing the cost of naming. We delist on notice because that is the only honest response to the cost being collected.

## What we do when an objection arrives

The protocol does not delist. The website can. A subject — or a verified representative, or a court order, or a credible non-consensual-content report — files the form documented in [Subject Removal Policy](/trust/subject-removal-policy). We send a verification link. We accept proof of identity, use it once, and discard it. We delist the market from nsgame.org permanently, across rounds, across redesigns. The chain still remembers; nobody, including us, can change that.

We do not pre-clear every market against every subject's right of publicity. The cost-of-enforcement asymmetry would make that work unaffordable to perform on every wallet's mint. We respond, instead, to every objection that arrives. A market without an objection runs. A market with one comes down. The asymmetry runs both ways: we cannot afford to clear, the subject cannot afford to sue every small pool — but a single takedown letter, free to write, costs us nothing to honor. That is the practical bargain. It is not virtue; it is geometry.

## What we are, in law

Three sentences, one frame each. The protocol is speech and a tool — *Bernstein v. US* established that publishing cryptographic source code is protected expression; *Coin Center v. Yellen* and *Van Loon v. Treasury* (5th Cir., Nov 2024) extended the doctrine to non-custodial smart-contract infrastructure. The website is a curated index of third-party submissions — Section 230(c)(1) of the Communications Decency Act and the *Force v. Facebook* line treat selection among third-party content as immune in many configurations. The wager is on a lawfully-obtained public metric — *Bartnicki v. Vopper*, *Florida Star v. B.J.F.*, and the transformative-use test from *Comedy III v. Saderup* describe the speech surface that wraps a public counter and a probabilistic instrument built on top of it.

State right-of-publicity statutes — California Civil Code §3344, New York Civil Rights §50–51, Tennessee's ELVIS Act, the Indiana, Ohio, and Washington analogues — reach name-only commercial use. We have not tested our posture against them. We operate as if we had no shield while preserving the arguments. Anti-SLAPP statutes — Cal. Civ. Proc. §425.16, the Texas TCPA, and parallel laws in roughly thirty US jurisdictions — shift fees onto plaintiffs whose suits target speech on matters of public concern. A plaintiff considering suit should know this is part of the geometry.

We have several plausible defences. We rest on none of them. We behave as if we had none.

## What we do for non-consensual content

The Take It Down Act (federal US, signed 19 May 2025) requires takedown of non-consensual intimate imagery within 48 hours of credible report. We do not host imagery. We do display markets that reference subjects whose underlying content may have been distributed without consent. The distinction is real and does not, by itself, exempt us.

Our SLA: any credible NCII report routes to a fast-track removal — within 48 hours of receipt, the named market is delisted from nsgame.org. The form is the same as the standard request in [Subject Removal Policy](/trust/subject-removal-policy); the priority flag is different. We do not require court orders for NCII reports. We require enough information to identify the subject and the market.

BIPA (740 ILCS 14), the Texas CUBI (Bus. & Com. Code §503.001), and the parallel biometric statutes are not implicated. The protocol processes only handles — strings — and counters — integers. No biometric identifier, no face template, no voice sample, no derived facial geometry. If that ever changes, this paragraph changes first.

A regulator's notice or a court order is honoured at the same speed.

## What this is not

This page does not claim a permission slip. We do not have one. No subject we name has consented to the market on themselves; consent was not sought because, under the protocol's current posture, it is not legally required for a public-metric wager — and that "not required" is a fact about the law's silence, not its blessing. We do not claim the law is settled. We do not claim our reading of right-of-publicity will survive its first judicial test. We do not claim that operating in legal grey is a moral position; it is a temporal one, and temporal positions expire.

We claim only this. The protocol is a neutral wagering rail. The website is a curated window onto it. The cost-of-enforcement geometry has, so far, kept that arrangement workable. The law has not yet ruled and may rule against us. When a real subject objects through the documented workflow, the website removes the market. The chain keeps its ledger; nobody will ever stop it from doing so, including us.

If that arrangement is unacceptable to you, the protocol does not need you, and the document you are reading does not need to convince you. It needs only to admit, exactly, what the protocol has chosen to admit — and no more.
