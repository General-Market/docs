# Hormones and how relationships shift — what the research actually says

> A cited, honest report for a layperson. Every claim below was run through a three-vote adversarial check; claims that failed are listed in "Refuted," not hidden. Sources are graded at the bottom.

---

## TL;DR — read this first

**No hormone level you could measure in a woman predicts whether your relationship will last or be happy.** That is the single most important finding, and it is the opposite of how hormones are sold online.

What the research *does* support, cleanly:

- **Hormones describe systems, not destinies.** Lust, attraction, and attachment are three separate brain systems with different chemistry. A relationship "shifts" as the balance between them moves — that is the real long-term arc.
- **Passion does not have to die.** In couples married ~21 years who still felt intense love, the brain's dopamine reward centres still lit up for the partner. What fades is *obsession*, not necessarily intensity.
- **The famous "fertile women crave manly men" claim is dead.** Large, preregistered, hormone-measured studies killed it. The only real cycle effect is small and non-selective.
- **"Oxytocin = the love hormone" is pop-science.** Voles genetically missing the oxytocin receptor still pair-bond. And a single oxytocin blood test is too unreliable to tell you anything about a person.
- **The one well-replicated hormone–relationship link is about men, not women:** partnered men and fathers carry modestly lower testosterone than single, childless men.

**What this report could NOT answer** (the research came back empty or thin — see [Scope gaps](#what-the-research-did-not-answer)): pregnancy/postpartum, hormonal birth control, menopause, prolactin, cortisol, and testosterone-and-libido *in women specifically*. Treat those as open, not as "no effect."

---

## The one frame to internalise

You came in with a reasonable instinct: testosterone tracks sex drive and risk-taking, so surely women's hormones are a dashboard you can read to forecast a relationship. The honest science says the dashboard does not exist.

Hormones operate at two levels. At the **population** level — thousands of people averaged — some hormone–behaviour links are real and replicated. At the **individual** level — *this* woman, *this* relationship, next year — hormone readings are noisy, unstable, and weakly predictive at best. The pop-science industry sells you population-level averages as if they were individual-level fortune-telling. They are not the same thing, and the gap is where almost every overblown claim lives.

Therefore: use hormones to understand *how love is built* — the systems, the arc, the life-stage shocks. Do not use them to predict *whether your specific bond survives*. For that, behaviour is the instrument, not chemistry.

The body sets the weather. The behaviour decides the climate.

## 1. Love is three systems, not one feeling

The durable foundation of this whole field is Helen Fisher's three-system model. Love is not one chemical event; it is three brain systems that evolved for three different jobs and run on different chemistry.

| System | Evolved job | Driven by | Feels like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lust** (sex drive) | Get you to mate with *any* suitable partner | Androgens (testosterone), oestrogens | Generalised horniness |
| **Attraction** (romantic love) | Focus your energy on *one* preferred partner | High dopamine, low serotonin | Obsession, craving, the "high" |
| **Attachment** (pair-bonding) | Keep you together long enough to raise young | Oxytocin, vasopressin | Calm, security, "home" |

> **Source.** Fisher, Aron, Mashek, Li & Brown (2002), *Archives of Sexual Behavior* 31(5):413–419, PMID 12238608. The three systems are "discrete, interrelated emotion-motivation systems… each associated with a specific constellation of neural correlates."

**Why this matters for "how a relationship shifts."** A relationship changes over time as the *weight* between these three systems moves — typically from attraction-dominant (the obsessive early high) toward attachment-dominant (the secure long bond). That migration *is* the long-term arc you are asking about.

**The honest caveat.** Fisher's own word is *interrelated*. The systems overlap and feed each other — testosterone can kick-start dopamine; orgasm releases oxytocin. fMRI work (Cacioppo 2012) confirms love and desire share brain regions as well as having distinct ones. So this is three *functions*, not three sealed boxes.

A relationship does not lose love when the obsession quiets. It changes which system is carrying the weight.

## 2. Does the passion have to die? No.

The most hopeful — and genuinely surprising — finding in the set: the dopamine "high" of early love can survive into very long marriages.

Researchers scanned 17 people married an average of 21 years who still reported being intensely in love. Looking at their partner's face activated the **same dopamine-rich reward regions** (the VTA and dorsal striatum) seen in brand-new lovers — *plus* the attachment regions tied to long-term bonding.

> **Source.** Acevedo, Aron, Fisher & Brown (2012), *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience* 7(2):145–159, PMID 21208991.

What actually fades is not intensity — it is **obsession**. The long-married still-in-love group scored *low* on obsessive, intrusive preoccupation. Early-stage lovers score high. So the thing that mellows is the anxious, can't-stop-thinking-about-them compulsion, not the reward itself.

**The honest caveats — and they are real:**

- **Tiny sample (N=17)** and self-selected for still-intense love. This shows persistence is *possible*, not that it is *typical*.
- **Cross-sectional, not longitudinal.** They did not follow the same couples for 21 years; they inferred "what fades" by comparing different groups. That is a weaker design than tracking.
- fMRI is correlational — lit-up regions are associations, not proof of cause.

Take the realistic reading: for some couples the reward system keeps firing for decades. It is not guaranteed, but "the spark must die" is *not* what the brain data says.

The fire can keep burning. It is the fever that breaks.

## 3. Oxytocin: the "cuddle hormone" story is broken

Oxytocin is sold everywhere as *the* bonding hormone — touch releases it, it glues couples together, more is better. The research has quietly dismantled this.

**The headline experiment.** Scientists used CRISPR to breed prairie voles (the classic monogamy animal) that genetically *lack a working oxytocin receptor*. The prediction was that they could not bond. Instead, they **bonded as strongly as normal voles** and parented normally.

> **Source.** Berendzen, Manoli et al. (2023), *Neuron* — titled, flatly, "Oxytocin receptor is not required for social attachment in prairie voles." Three independent receptor-null lines, all bonding normally.

This overturned a 30-year inference. Earlier studies that *blocked* oxytocin with drugs and saw bonding fail had been read as "oxytocin is necessary." The genetic knockout says: the system is more redundant and more complicated than that.

**The balanced view (this is important — don't overcorrect into "oxytocin does nothing"):**

- A peer-reviewed critique (Danoff, Whelan & Connelly) argues the knockouts don't prove the receptor is *fully* dispensable — there could be developmental compensation. The *fact* (mutants bond) is uncontested; the *interpretation* is debated.
- A 2024 follow-up shows the receptor still **modulates** bonding — it speeds up bond formation and suppresses post-bond promiscuity — even when knockouts can bond without it.

**Net:** oxytocin *shapes* bonding; it is not a simple on/off switch for love. This is exactly why human oxytocin nasal-spray trials give such mixed, disappointing results.

## 4. You cannot meaningfully measure someone's oxytocin

Even if oxytocin mattered exactly as the myth claims, you still could not use it to read a person — because the measurement itself is unreliable.

When researchers measured the same people's oxytocin twice, the test-retest agreement was near-worthless: an intraclass correlation of **0.23 in saliva and 0.29 in plasma** (1.0 is perfect, 0 is random). Within the same person, levels swung by **57–63%** — over four times the assay's technical error, meaning the instability is *real biology*, not lab sloppiness.

> **Source.** Martins et al. (2020), *eLife* 62456. Conclusion: single baseline oxytocin measurements are *not* valid trait markers.

**What this kills:** any idea that you could take a partner's oxytocin reading and infer their "bonding capacity" or how attached they are to you. One snapshot tells you almost nothing about the person. (Scope note: this particular study used male participants and one assay method; the authors limit their claim accordingly — but it converges with a decade of measurement-skepticism literature.)

## 5. Oxytocin and exclusivity — a weak, single-study hint

There is *one* intriguing finding worth flagging precisely because it is weak, so you weight it correctly.

A small placebo-controlled study (n=44, 91% women) found intranasal oxytocin promoted pair-bond **exclusivity** partly by making people socially *distance* from attractive strangers — and the effect was **stronger in newer relationships**, hinting at a "sensitive period" for forming attachment. Separately, a 2013 study found oxytocin made partnered men rate *their own* partner (not other women) as more attractive, lighting up reward regions.

> **Source.** Freeman/Scheele et al. (2021), *Psychoneuroendocrinology* 134, PMID 34607172; Scheele et al. (2013), *PNAS*.

**Why you should hold this loosely:**

- n=44, single study, no independent replication found.
- The "sensitive period" rests on a cross-sectional comparison, not on tracking couples over time.
- The whole field of intranasal-oxytocin social effects has a **poor replication record** — its famous "trust" and "mind-reading" effects failed high-powered preregistered re-tests.
- One related sub-claim (that oxytocin reduces attachment to mothers and other figures) was **outright refuted** in this report's verification (0-of-3 vote).

Interesting direction. Not something to build a belief on.

## 6. The big debunk: "fertile women want manly men" did NOT replicate

This is the most important correction in the entire report, because it is the most repeated claim in online "hormone" content.

**The claim ("ovulatory shift" / "dual mating" hypothesis):** when a woman is fertile (mid-cycle), her preferences shift toward more masculine, dominant, "good genes" men — implying women are wired to seek a provider while sneaking better DNA when ovulating.

**The verdict: it failed.** Two large, **preregistered, within-subject** studies (N=157 each, two cycles tracked, actual salivary hormones measured, ovulation confirmed by urine tests — designed specifically to fix the sloppy methods of earlier work) found **no such shift**:

- Stern, Gerlach & Penke (2020), *Psychological Science*: "women's mate preferences for men's behaviors did not shift across the cycle." Every hormone-by-behaviour interaction was non-significant.
- Jünger et al. (2018), *Evolution and Human Behavior*: "our results do not support women's mate preference shifts, as assumed by the good genes ovulatory shift hypothesis."

Backed by a 2014 meta-analysis (58 studies, no short-term good-genes preference) and a 2020 longitudinal study (N=257, "no compelling robust evidence").

> **Sources.** Stern et al. 2020 (Psychological Science); Jünger et al. 2018 (Evolution and Human Behavior).

If you have absorbed the idea that women's attraction swings toward "alpha" men when they are fertile — that idea did not survive the best studies. Drop it.

## 7. What the menstrual cycle *actually* does

The cycle is not inert — but the real effect is small and unglamorous.

The one reliable finding: when fertile, women rate **all men as slightly more attractive** — for both short-term flings *and* long-term partners — **regardless of how masculine** the men are. It is a small, general lift in openness/desire, not a targeted hunt for better genes.

> **Source.** Stern et al. 2020: the rise was γ=0.10, 95% CI [0.01, 0.20], p=.035 — "the effect seems to be rather small and the lower bound of the CI is near 0."

**Honest extra caveat:** even this small effect was *conditional* in one study (Jünger 2018) — it showed up in partnered women, not single women. So the single surviving cycle effect is both small and fragile.

Practical translation: a fertile partner may feel marginally more drawn to people in general. There is no evidence she is being selectively pulled toward "more masculine" men.

## 8. Testosterone and bonding — the one solid link is about men

The best-replicated hormone–relationship finding in the entire set is not about women at all. It is about you.

A meta-analysis of **114 effects from 66 studies** found that **pair-bonded men have lower testosterone than single men, and fathers have lower testosterone than childless men.** Men more oriented toward bonding and parenting carry less.

> **Source.** Grebe, Sarafin, Strenth & Zilioli (2019), *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, PMID 30639674. Supported by Gettler et al. (2011, *PNAS*), which found fatherhood *causally* lowers testosterone over time.

**Read the direction, not the magnitude.** The effect sizes are small, there is evidence of publication bias, and most studies are cross-sectional. What is robust is the *direction*: settling into a committed bond and into fatherhood pulls a man's testosterone down. The body reallocates from mating effort to bonding effort. This is real, and it is the cleanest hormone story here.

The thing the research could *not* confirm: testosterone driving sociosexuality or libido **in women specifically**. That link is plausible and often claimed, but no verified study in this set substantiated it. See the next section.

## Refuted — do not repeat these

Three popular-sounding claims were actively *killed* by the verification step. Naming them protects you from repeating them:

1. **The "vasopressin commitment gene" in men (AVPR1A RS3 / the 334 allele).** The widely-cited claim that men carrying a certain vasopressin-receptor gene variant bond less and have more marital trouble **did not survive verification** (voted down 2-to-1). The original Walum et al. study is real, but the finding is contested and did not hold up in this evidence set. Do not present it as established.
2. **"Oxytocin nasal spray reduces attachment to your mother and other figures."** Refuted 3-to-0.
3. **"The vole study wasn't a real genetic knockout."** Overstated and refuted — the mutants are genuine receptor-null animals. The legitimate critique is the milder "we can't be *certain* the receptor is fully dispensable."

## The reframe: what actually predicts a relationship's future

If hormones don't predict your relationship's trajectory, what does? **Behaviour.** This is where the predictive science actually lives.

The longest-running predictive work on relationships (Gottman's newlywed studies) forecasts divorce from how couples *behave* — conflict patterns, contempt, the ratio of warm to hostile moments, repair attempts — with accuracy no hormone test approaches. The broader literature points to three behavioural predictors that hold up:

- **Commitment level** — explicit investment in a shared future.
- **Conflict and communication style** — *how* you fight, not whether you fight.
- **Perceived partner responsiveness** — does each person feel understood, validated, cared for.

> **Source.** Gottman & colleagues, "Predicting Marital Happiness and Stability from Newlywed Interactions."

**One honesty flag:** this report did not surface a *direct head-to-head* study putting hormone predictors and behavioural predictors in the same model. So "behaviour beats hormones for predicting outcomes" is a well-grounded synthesis inference from the two literatures — strongly supported by their relative track records, but not a single citation.

The instrument that reads a relationship's future is behaviour. Watch how she treats you in a disagreement, not what her hormones are doing.

## Practical takeaways for you

1. **Stop trying to read her hormones. The dashboard isn't real.** No assay predicts your relationship. This is settled, and it frees you to look at what matters.
2. **Understand the arc, not the chemistry.** The shift from obsessive early "attraction" to steady "attachment" is normal and healthy — the obsession fading is not love fading.
3. **Passion fading is not inevitable.** Some long bonds keep the reward system firing. Novelty, shared challenge, and physical closeness feed it.
4. **Ignore "fertile women want alpha males" content entirely.** It is the single most debunked claim in the field.
5. **Your own biology shifts too.** Committing and fathering lower your testosterone — that is the body backing the bond, not a loss.
6. **Judge the relationship by behaviour.** Commitment, how you both handle conflict, whether each feels heard. That is the real predictor — and unlike hormones, it is something you can actually change.

## Glossary

- **Pair-bond** — a durable, preferential attachment to one partner.
- **Lust / attraction / attachment** — the three brain systems of love (sex drive / romantic obsession / long-term bonding).
- **Oxytocin & vasopressin** — peptide hormones tied to bonding and attachment.
- **Dopamine** — reward/craving neurotransmitter; engine of the early-love "high."
- **Testosterone** — primary androgen; tracks sex drive and (at population level) mating-vs-bonding orientation.
- **Ovulatory shift hypothesis** — the (now non-replicated) claim that fertile women shift preference toward masculine men.
- **Sociosexuality** — how open a person is to casual, uncommitted sex.
- **Preregistered study** — hypotheses and analysis locked in *before* data collection, which prevents cherry-picking; the gold standard here.
- **Within-subject design** — the same people compared to themselves over time, far stronger than comparing different groups.
- **Cross-sectional** — a single-snapshot comparison of different groups; weaker for claims about change over time.
- **Test-retest reliability (ICC)** — whether a measurement gives the same answer twice; near 0 = useless, near 1 = trustworthy.
- **Intranasal oxytocin** — oxytocin given as a nasal spray in experiments; its social effects replicate poorly.
- **AVPR1A** — the vasopressin-receptor gene behind the contested "male commitment gene" claim.
