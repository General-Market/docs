# Sectioned Scoring Evaluation

## Dimensions (each scored 0-10)

Each dimension measures one independent quality axis. When scoring, ask: "If I changed ONLY this aspect and nothing else, would the script improve?" If yes, the dimension is measuring something real.

**Independence test**: After scoring, check for suspicious co-movement. If two dimensions both moved in the same direction by the same amount after a single change, verify that each improvement has its OWN evidence. Common traps: Narrative Arc and Rhythm both improving from a reorder (the reorder may have improved sequencing but not time-use — score only the one that genuinely changed), Visual Narrative and Production Direction both improving from a visual note (the note may specify a visual argument but not a craft specification, or vice versa).

- **Opening Hook** measures the FIRST IMPRESSION — the survival test.
- **Narrative Arc** measures SEQUENCING — does the order matter?
- **Rhythm & Pacing** measures TIME — how duration and silence are used.
- **Voice** measures IDENTITY — would this be mistaken for someone else's work?
- **Clarity** measures UNDERSTANDING — does the audience get it?
- **Visual Narrative** measures the IMAGE TRACK — what the eyes tell, independent of words.
- **Closing Power** measures the LAST IMPRESSION — the reason to share or act. (Distinct from Narrative Arc: a script can have a perfect arc with a forgettable ending, or a weak arc with a devastating final line.)
- **Production Direction** measures CRAFT SPECIFICATIONS — can a producer execute this excellently?

### 1. OPENING HOOK
- 0: No hook. Opens with context, explanation, or throat-clearing. The audience must wait to understand why they should care.
- 3: Hook exists but is generic ("What if you could...") or cliché for the format. The audience has seen this opening before.
- 5: Hook is specific to the product/topic but doesn't create urgency. The audience understands the subject but isn't compelled to stay.
- 7: Hook creates genuine curiosity — a question forms in the audience's mind that only the rest of the video can answer. They would not look away, skip, or check their phone.
- 10: Hook is so unexpected the audience physically leans forward. It works even without context on who made it. The hook itself is worth sharing.

### 2. NARRATIVE ARC
- 0: Ideas or sections are random — rearranging them changes nothing.
- 3: Some ordering exists but the viewer wouldn't notice if two sections swapped.
- 5: Clear progression in one dimension (simple → complex, small → large, mundane → exotic). Swapping the first and last would feel wrong.
- 7: Multi-dimensional progression — the argument, the emotion, and the stakes all escalate together. Each beat exists because of the one before it.
- 10: The sequence feels inevitable in retrospect. Every element is load-bearing. Removing one collapses the arc.

### 3. RHYTHM & PACING
- 0: Monotonous — every beat is the same length and energy. No variation in sentence length, section duration, or intensity.
- 3: Some variation exists but feels accidental — the script doesn't know it has rhythm.
- 5: Intentional rhythm with at least one clear tempo change or deliberate pause.
- 7: Musical — fast sections contrast with slow, short sentences alternate with long, silence is used as punctuation. The reader can FEEL the acceleration and deceleration.
- 10: The pacing itself argues. Speed conveys urgency, deceleration conveys weight, silence conveys significance. Remove the words and the rhythm alone communicates.

### 4. VOICE (fidelity to specified voice)
- 0: Generic corporate. Reads like a pitch deck or product page. No personality.
- 3: Attempts the voice but it's performative — the reader senses effort rather than conviction.
- 5: Good prose with personality, but it could belong to any brand with a similar attitude.
- 7: Distinctive and consistent — the voice is unmistakable, not an overlay but the substrate. Every sentence sounds like it was written by someone who means it.
- 10: Sentences that make you stop reading. The voice is not just present but load-bearing — rewriting any line in a neutral voice would lose meaning, not just style.

### 5. CLARITY
- 0: The target audience would understand nothing.
- 3: The product/concept is vaguely clear but the value prop is not.
- 5: The target audience gets what it is within 30 seconds.
- 7: The target audience gets what it is AND why it matters within 30 seconds.
- 10: Crystal clear AND nothing is over-explained. Every word earns its place. Clarity is achieved THROUGH the voice, not despite it — the voice is the vehicle for understanding, not an obstacle to it.

### 6. VISUAL NARRATIVE
- 0: No visual thinking. The script assumes words carry everything.
- 3: Generic visual cues ("show the product", "cut to logo"). A producer would need to invent the visual language.
- 5: Specific visuals per section, but they illustrate the words rather than adding to them. The visuals are subtitles for the ears.
- 7: Visuals carry their own argument — they communicate meaning that the words do not state. Techniques: visual metaphor, data overlay, juxtaposition, progressive reveal, or a running visual element that accumulates meaning.
- 10: On mute (or with slides only, for talks), the visual sequence alone tells a coherent story. Words and visuals are two independent tracks that converge at the closing.

### 7. CLOSING POWER
- 0: Fizzles out, trails off, or ends with a generic CTA ("Sign up today!").
- 3: Has a clear ending but it's just a label — no emotional weight, no callback, no reframe.
- 5: Clean ending with a single CTA. Competent but forgettable — the viewer nods and moves on.
- 7: The ending recontextualizes everything before it. The viewer re-evaluates what they just watched. There is a callback, a reframe, or a reversal.
- 10: The closing is the reason someone shares the video. It answers a question the viewer didn't know they had. Cutting the last 15 seconds would make the whole script worse, not just shorter.

### 8. PRODUCTION DIRECTION
Measures the CRAFT SPECIFICATIONS — not what the script says or how it flows (those are other dimensions), but whether a producer has enough direction to make it excellently.
- 0: Script is words only. No guidance on how to produce it.
- 3: Mentions format (aspect ratio, duration) but nothing about sound, type, or color.
- 5: Includes format, visual treatment notes, and basic production context. A producer could make it without guessing.
- 7: Sound design, typography, color palette, and delivery method are specified with intention. A producer would make it WELL because the script tells them WHY each choice matters.
- 10: Every production specification reinforces the message. The sound argues. The typography argues. The color argues. The format argues. A different producer reading this script would make the same core choices.

## The 7-to-10 Gap
Most scripts will spend their lives between 7 and 10. This is where discrimination matters most.

- **7** = "This works and I wouldn't be embarrassed by it." Professional. Competent. The audience nods.
- **8** = "This is genuinely good." The audience remembers it tomorrow. There's at least one moment of surprise or delight.
- **9** = "This is remarkable." The audience tells someone about it. Multiple moments land with force. Hard to imagine a specific improvement.
- **10** = "I cannot imagine this being better for this audience, this format, this purpose." Not perfection in the abstract — perfection for the context. You can imagine a DIFFERENT script being a 10, but not a better VERSION of this script.

**The diagnostic ladder**:
- If you can articulate what a 9 looks like for a given dimension but the script isn't there yet, score it **8**.
- If you cannot articulate what would make it better, it's a **9**.
- If you've tried to make it better and failed, it might be a **10**.
- If you're unsure between two adjacent scores, choose the lower one. Generosity is inflation.

## Cross-Cutting Concerns
These affect multiple dimensions and should be considered during scoring:

- **Duration fit**: Is the script right-sized for its format? A 15s social cut with 45s of content fails at Rhythm, Clarity, AND Production Direction. A 5min talk with 60s of material fails at Narrative Arc and Rhythm. Score the relevant dimensions lower if the content doesn't fit the container.
- **Audience calibration**: Is the complexity, jargon level, and assumed knowledge appropriate for the target audience? This affects Clarity primarily but also Voice (register should match audience expectations) and Opening Hook (what surprises a developer is different from what surprises a consumer).
- **Emotional throughline**: Does the audience feel something — surprise, recognition, conviction, urgency — at specific moments? This isn't a separate dimension because it emerges from the combination of Voice (makes you feel), Narrative Arc (builds tension), and Closing Power (pays it off). But if ALL three score 7+ and the script still doesn't move you, something is broken in how they combine.
- **Internal consistency**: Does the script contradict itself? If the header says "No voiceover" but the body has narration, if the format says "15 seconds" but the content reads as 90, if the tone section says "dark humor" but the script is earnest — these contradictions undermine every dimension. Score Production Direction and Clarity lower when the script fights itself.

## Scoring
Final score = average of all 8 dimensions (0-10 scale)

## Rules
- Score each dimension independently with a 1-sentence justification.
- The evaluator gives specific line references for high and low scores.
- The agent modifying the script CANNOT see or modify this file.
- A single change should move at most 2 dimensions. If a change appears to improve 3+ dimensions simultaneously, re-evaluate — at least one of those improvements is likely inflation, not genuine quality gain.
- **Independent justification**: Each dimension score must have its own justification that references DIFFERENT evidence from the script. If two dimensions' justifications reference the same line or section, they may be measuring the same thing — re-examine whether the score change is genuine for both.
- **The read-aloud test**: Before finalizing any score above 7, mentally read the relevant section aloud. Does it land? Does it feel right? Scoring should reflect the FELT quality of the work, not a checklist. A script can satisfy every anchor description and still be mediocre if the sentences don't land when spoken.
- Score 10 means "I cannot imagine this being better." If you can imagine a better version, it is not a 10. Reserve 10 for the unreasonable.
- **Substance over presence**: A production note that says "the typography should be bold" scores lower than one that says "the font weight increases across the montage from Light to Bold because the visual pressure should build with the narrative." Anchor descriptions reward SPECIFICITY and INTENTIONALITY, not the mere presence of the feature they describe. If a section technically matches an anchor but lacks depth, score one level below.
- **Voice floor**: If Voice scores below 7, the final average is capped at 7.0 regardless of other dimensions. Voice is not a dimension that can be traded away — it is a constraint. A clear, well-structured, beautifully paced script in the wrong voice is the wrong script.
- **Voice cross-check**: When scoring ANY dimension above 7, ask: "Does this score assume the current voice? Would the same score apply if the voice were corporate?" If the answer is "yes, same score either way," then voice has been eroded — the prose has become voice-neutral, which means it has lost its voice. This applies to ALL dimensions, not just text-heavy ones — even Visual Narrative and Production Direction should reflect the voice's sensibility in how they direct the viewer's experience.
