# Batch AB: Prediction Markets & Betting — Insider/Informed Trader Extraction

## PM011 — Ottaviani & Sorensen 2009, "Surprised by the Parimutuel Odds?"

Theoretical model of simultaneous parimutuel betting with private information. Argues the favorite-longshot bias (FLB) emerges because privately informed bettors cannot revise positions after observing final odds — they are "surprised" by the market probability. The bias is a Bayes-Nash equilibrium artifact: high market probability reveals favorable private info, but bettors cannot exploit this ex post. No quantitative estimates of insider extraction or adverse selection costs. The paper is purely structural — it identifies the mechanism by which private information distorts market prices, but does not measure the magnitude of wealth transfer from uninformed to informed participants.

## PM012 — Ottaviani & Sorensen 2010, "Noise, Information, and the Favorite-Longshot Bias"

Extended version of PM011 with multiple outcomes. Key structural result: the direction of the FLB depends on the ratio of private information to noise. When noise dominates (lotteries), a *reverse* FLB appears — popular numbers yield lower expected returns. When private information dominates (horse racing), the standard FLB emerges. The parimutuel structure has a "built-in insurance against adverse selection" not present in fixed-odds markets: more informed bettors drive odds to extremes, *reducing* the FLB in parimutuel but *increasing* it in fixed-odds. No quantitative extraction estimates. The theoretical contribution is the comparative static: more informed bettors worsen adverse selection in fixed-odds markets but ameliorate it in parimutuel markets.

## PM013 — Ottaviani & Sorensen 2003/2005, "Noise, Information, and the Favorite-Longshot Bias" (earlier working paper)

Earlier version of PM012 with a key empirical implication: late bets contain more information about finishing order than early bets, consistent with Asch, Malkiel and Quandt (1982). Informed bettors have an incentive to wait until post time and bet simultaneously, concentrating private information in last-minute wagers. Poorly informed bettors rationally abstain when recreational value is low, amplifying the FLB. The parimutuel payoff structure provides automatic insurance against adverse selection — unlike fixed-odds markets where the bookmaker bears the full cost. No quantitative extraction percentages, but the timing prediction (insiders bet late) is empirically supported.

## PM014 — Whelan 2024, "On Estimates of Insider Trading in Sports Betting"

Critical paper. Directly challenges the Shin (1993) z-measure used to estimate insider fractions. Key findings: (1) Applying Shin's method to professional tennis and European soccer data yields estimates of 3-6% of money placed by insiders — but Whelan argues these estimates are artifacts. (2) The z-measure is only accurate under the counterfactual assumption that bookmakers earn zero gross profits. With realistic profit margins (e.g., Paddy Power/Flutter reports), the method falsely reports positive insider fractions even when none exist. (3) For a two-contestant event, z estimates equal roughly beta-1 (the overround minus one) at 50/50 odds — meaning an overround of 1.05 mechanically produces z=0.05. (4) Higher bookmaker margins produce higher z estimates, not because of more insiders, but because the zero-profit assumption is violated. The z-measure is really measuring bookmaker margin, not insider activity. Devastating for the entire Shin-based insider estimation literature.

## PM014b — Fingleton & Waldron 1999, "Optimal Determination of Bookmakers' Betting Odds"

Extends Shin's model to incorporate bookmaker risk aversion and anti-competitive behavior. Using 1,696 Irish races from 1993: (1) Bookmakers are infinitely risk-averse (they balance books rather than maximize expected profit). (2) Operating costs and monopoly rents combined account for up to 4% of turnover. (3) Between 3.1% and 3.7% of betting (by value) comes from punters with inside information. (4) Cannot distinguish inside information from operating costs — the two are observationally equivalent in the model. The Irish overround per runner (3.33%) is nearly double the British figure (1.86%), which could reflect more insider trading, higher margins, or greater risk aversion. The 3.1-3.7% insider estimate is the most cited quantitative figure from this literature, though PM014 (Whelan) casts serious doubt on its validity.

## PM016 — Ziemba 2020, "Parimutuel Betting Markets: Racetracks and Lotteries Revisited"

Comprehensive survey. Key quantitative data: (1) Transaction costs are 13-30% for horseracing, ~50% for lotteries. Rebate shops reduce effective take to 10-13% for large bettors. (2) In 1986 California data, betting favorites at US odds of 3/10 or less yielded ~3% profit; at 100/1, fair odds were ~700/1, so longshot bets returned only 13.7 cents per dollar. In more recent data, favorites are no longer sufficiently underbet to be profitable. (3) Corporate insiders in financial markets "have private information that is not fully reflected in current prices." (4) The FLB is stronger late in race days as losing bettors chase losses on longshots. (5) Syndicates and professional bettors exploit cross-track arbitrage and exotic bet mispricing. No direct measurement of insider extraction percentage in betting markets, but documents the ecosystem of informed vs. uninformed participants.

## PM020 — Snowberg & Wolfers 2010, "Explaining the Favorite-Longshot Bias: Is it Risk-Love or Misperceptions?"

Uses a large US horse racing dataset (1992-2001). Key quantitative finding: rate of return on horses at 100/1+ odds is approximately -61%; random betting yields -23%; always betting the favorite loses only 5.5%. The paper tests whether FLB is driven by risk-love (rational bettors overweight longshots for the thrill) or probability misperception (Prospect Theory weighting). Evidence favors misperception: models that explain win-pool behavior cannot rationalize exacta/quinella/trifecta behavior under risk-love, but Prospect Theory's probability weighting function fits both. No direct insider extraction estimates — the paper is about *why* uninformed bettors misprice, not how much insiders extract from them. The -61% to -5.5% return gradient quantifies the cost of being wrong about probabilities.

## PM022 — Levitt 2004, "How Do Markets Function? Gambling on the NFL"

Key paper for market maker extraction. Uses ~20,000 NFL wagers from 285 bettors. Findings: (1) Bookmakers do NOT equalize money on both sides — in nearly half of games, 2/3+ of bets fall on one side. (2) Bookmakers strategically set "wrong" prices to exploit bettor biases (preference for favorites and visiting teams). (3) This strategy increases gross profit margins by 20-30% over a balanced-book approach. (4) Favorites and home teams win less than 50% of the time, yet attract more than half the action. (5) A bettor must win 52.4% of bets to overcome the 10% vig. (6) No evidence of systematically skilled bettors — distribution of outcomes is consistent with coin tosses. (7) Much of bookmaker organizational structure is "designed to protect the bookmaker against adverse selection by talented bettors" (citing Strumpf 2002). The bookmaker IS the informed party here, extracting from biased bettors rather than the reverse.

## PM024 — Forsythe, Rietz & Ross 1999, "Wishes, Expectations and Actions: Price Formation in Election Stock Markets"

Studies Iowa Political Stock Market. Individual traders exhibit systematic "wishful thinking" bias — they overvalue contracts for candidates they prefer. Despite this, market prices aggregate information efficiently and predict election outcomes well. Oliven and Rietz (1995) found substantial arbitrage opportunities that could not arise with rational traders. The mechanism: a few "marginal" rational traders are sufficient to correct prices even when most participants are biased. No quantitative estimates of informed trader extraction. The relevance is structural: prediction markets function despite widespread participant irrationality because marginal informed traders discipline prices.

## PM025 — Berg & Rietz 2003, "Prediction Markets as Decision Support Systems"

Describes Iowa Electronic Markets as decision support tools. Focuses on 1996 Presidential election conditional prediction markets. Republicans could have inferred from market prices that Dole was a weak candidate. No data on insider trading, adverse selection, or informed trader extraction. The paper is about using prediction market prices for decision-making, not about the microstructure of who profits from whom. NO RELEVANT DATA for insider extraction questions.
