# Batch 04 — Prediction Market Papers (Prediction Market Subfield)

## PM020 — Snowberg & Wolfers (2010), "Explaining the Favorite-Longshot Bias: Is it Risk-Love or Misperceptions?"

- **Focus:** Tests whether the favorite-longshot bias in horse racing is driven by risk-love (utility curvature) or probability misperceptions (Prospect Theory weighting). Uses 6.4M horse starts from US races 1992-2001.
- **Favorite-longshot bias quantified:** Betting on horses at 100/1 odds yields returns of ~-61%. Betting randomly yields -23%. Betting the favorite yields losses of only -5.5%. The bias has persisted globally for 50+ years across pari-mutuel and bookmaker markets.
- **Misperceptions dominate:** The misperceptions class (probability weighting function, Prelec parameter a=0.928) outperforms risk-love in predicting exotic bet prices. For exactas, misperceptions is 18.4% closer to actual prices (mean absolute % error: 28.0% vs 34.3%). For trifectas, misperceptions wins 71.1% of observation-level comparisons vs 28.9% for risk-love.
- **No informed-trader extraction data.** The paper does not measure insider/informed trader share of value extracted, adverse selection costs, or market maker losses. It is entirely about the structural bias in probability weighting by the aggregate bettor population.
- **Relevance to insider question:** The persistent -5.5% to -61% return gradient implies that any informed bettor who can correctly assess probabilities faces an environment where the uninformed systematically misprice longshots. The cost of misperception to the average bettor is "very large," and de-biasing could "reduce their cost of gambling substantially" — but no quantitative extraction rate is given.

## PM022 — Levitt (2004), "How Do Markets Function? An Empirical Analysis of Gambling on the National Football League"

- **Focus:** Analyzes NFL sports betting using ~20,000 wagers from 285 bettors in a handicapping contest at an online sportsbook (2001-02 season). Unique dataset with both prices AND quantities.
- **Bookmaker profit model:** E[profit] = (2+v)(f+p-2pf) - 1, where v=vig (10%), f=fraction bet on favorite, p=probability favorite wins. If bettors are biased (f(p=0.5) > 0.5), bookmaker maximizes by setting p < 0.5.
- **Bettor bias quantified:** 60.6% of bets placed on favorites overall. Visiting favorites attract 68.2% of bets. Home underdogs attract only 31.8%. Three-fourths of all contestants chose favorites more often than underdogs.
- **Bookmaker exploitation:** Favorites win only 48.2% of games over 21 NFL seasons (4,793 games). Visiting favorites win only 46.7%. Given the betting distribution, bettors win 49.45% of bets. At standard 110/100 vig, this raises bookmaker gross profit rate from 5.0% (at balanced 50/50) to 6.16% — a **23% increase in gross profits** from exploiting bettor bias. Over 250 games/season, the bookmaker's expected profit std dev is only 2.5%, meaning negative gross profits occur less than once per 100 seasons.
- **No skilled bettors found:** Distribution of bettor outcomes is indistinguishable from random coin flips (p=0.50). No persistence in win rates — past success has zero predictive value for future performance. Cumulative ranking coefficients are jointly insignificant (p-values 0.51 to 0.73).
- **Bookmaker as informed actor:** "A fundamental difference between gambling and financial markets is that it is possible in gambling to find and hire a small set of individuals (the odds makers) who can systematically do better in predicting game outcomes than can bettors overall." Horse racing, with extreme insider info, uses pari-mutuel (fixed house take) precisely because insiders exist.
- **Adverse selection defense:** Strumpf (2002) finds that "much of the internal structure of bookmaker organizations is designed to protect the bookmaker against adverse selection by these talented bettors." Local bookmakers offer different odds to bettors with different past histories (price discrimination against skilled bettors).

## PM024 — Forsythe, Rietz & Ross (1999), "Wishes, Expectations and Actions: Price Formation in Election Stock Markets"

- **Focus:** How do election stock markets (Iowa Electronic Markets) produce accurate predictions despite biased individual traders? Studies 1988 US Presidential, 1993 Canadian Federal Election markets.
- **Wishful thinking bias quantified:** In 12 US presidential elections (1952-1996), 71-84% of voters who prefer a candidate also expect that candidate to win. In the 1993 Canadian market, supporters held portfolio shares of their preferred party **3x larger** than the market average (e.g., NDP supporters held 17.4% in NDP contracts vs 4.9% market average; Reform supporters held 35.8% vs 16.7%).
- **Trading bias:** In the 1988 market, Bush supporters bought net 1.12 Bush shares/day and sold 2.26 Dukakis shares/day. Dukakis supporters bought 2.01 Dukakis and sold 1.59 Bush. Both statistically significant.
- **Marginal trader effect:** Despite pervasive individual bias, *marginal traders* (those setting prices via limit orders near market) show NO evidence of judgment bias. Non-marginal Bush supporters buy significantly more Bush stock; marginal Bush supporters show no such bias (t-stat 0.24, insignificant). Market makers err at 1/6 the rate of price takers.
- **Market accuracy despite bias:** Average absolute prediction error across 106 contracts in 20 markets = 2.39%. Large North American markets = 1.75% error. Markets outperform final polls.
- **Mistakes are frequent:** In the 1992 US Presidential Market, 17% of all market-making and price-taking orders violated weak rationality. Average violation cost $0.097. Price-taking violations in 34.4% of price-taking orders (avg loss $0.104). Market-making violations in 5.8% of orders (avg loss $0.071).
- **No insider extraction rate.** The paper documents that biased traders lose money to unbiased marginal traders, but does not quantify a % extraction rate. The key finding is that a small number of rational price-setters dominate pricing despite a biased majority.

## PM025 — Berg & Rietz (2003), "Prediction Markets as Decision Support Systems"

- **Focus:** Uses 1996 IEM conditional prediction markets to demonstrate decision support — could Republicans have identified Dole as a weak nominee and chosen a stronger candidate?
- **Powell effect:** Correlation between Powell's nomination probability and Clinton's winning probability = -0.60. Regression: each 1% increase in Powell nomination likelihood decreased Clinton's win probability by 0.044%. Powell's withdrawal moved markets by 21.43 standard deviations (nomination) and 4.66 standard deviations (Clinton win).
- **Dole weakness:** Correlation between Dole's nomination probability and Clinton's win = +0.55. Each 1% increase in Dole's nomination probability increased Clinton's win probability by 0.066%. Clinton's average forecast winning margin over Dole = 5.18% (actual margin = 9.2%).
- **Conditional market trading volume:** ~9,000 contracts over 39 trading days, averaging 222/day across all conditional pairs.
- **NO RELEVANT DATA** on insider/informed trader extraction. This paper is entirely about using prediction market prices for decision-making, not about information asymmetry, adverse selection, or informed trader returns.

## PM034 — Cowgill & Zitzewitz (2015), "Corporate Prediction Markets: Evidence from Google, Ford, and Firm X"

- **Focus:** Efficiency and inefficiencies in corporate prediction markets at Google (270 markets, 1,465 traders, 70,706 trades), Ford (149 markets, 902 traders, 9,258 trades), and Firm X (1,345 markets, 57 traders, 12,655 trades).
- **Markets beat experts:** Prediction markets achieve up to 25% lower MSE than expert forecasts (Ford sales: MSE ratio 0.742, p=0.104; Google: 0.727, p=0.00004; Firm X: 0.924, p=0.002).
- **Insider trading in corporate prediction markets:** Insiders (project team members + friends) account for 10% of trades at Google. Insiders are MORE likely to trade optimistically (buy their own project's success). Insiders' trades are NOT systematically profitable — they earn LOWER returns. "Those with the most knowledge of a market's subject [trade] in an unprofitable (and potentially strategically biased) way."
- **Counter-insiders profit:** Programmers and employees at Mountain View/New York headquarters "tend to trade against biases and earn higher returns." More experienced traders and those with higher past returns trade pessimistically (against the optimism bias) and profit. Traders with high past returns earn high future returns (persistent skill).
- **Optimism bias:** Google markets exhibit optimism bias (negative average returns from overpriced optimistic securities), strongest in project-completion markets — outcomes most under employees' control. A 2% increase in Google stock price (1 std dev) is associated with optimistic securities being priced 3-4 percentage points higher (mood swings). New hires are the most optimistic.
- **Biases diminish over time:** At Google and Ford Sales, initial pricing biases disappear by end of sample. More experienced traders trade against biases. Unskilled traders exit (attrition correlates with poor past performance at Google and Ford). Firm X markets were well-calibrated from the start (57 experienced traders, avg 220 trades each over ~5 years).
- **Bias toward naive priors reversed:** Contrary to the Ali/Manski longshot bias, Google and Ford Sales markets exhibit overreaction to new information (prices move AWAY from 1/N priors). This is "consistent with an overreaction to new information or with participants underappreciating the effort that was put into security design."
- **Companies suppress bad news:** Four companies shut down prediction markets after they forecast problems with projects. "Agents, including CEOs, may prefer noisier measures of performance, especially if performance is expected to be disappointing."
