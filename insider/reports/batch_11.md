## PM161 — Hanson (2000) "Shall We Vote on Values, But Bet on Beliefs?"

**Focus:** Proposal for "futarchy" — using prediction/betting markets to guide government policy decisions. Theoretical/institutional design paper.

**Relevant data (insider/informed trading dynamics):**
- Parimutuel betting markets typically charge ~18% transaction fee. This high cost creates the "long-shot bias" — over-estimating chances of unlikely outcomes — because those favoring low-probability outcomes spend proportionally less to move prices, and transaction costs deter arbitrageurs from correcting the bias.
- "Market makers" in election betting markets (IEM) were found to be unbiased on average. Compared to other traders: invest 2x as much, trade more frequently, earn higher returns, and make 1/6 as many errors. More highly educated, more experienced. These informed participants drive price accuracy through disproportionate influence.
- Speculative markets put "if anything, too much weight on advice from experts, both public and private" (citing Figlewski 1979, Metzger 1985).
- Re: manipulation by insiders/interested parties: "the only way for such an interested party to substantially influence the price is to possibly have substantial special information, or to trade via parties that might have such information." Speculators with deep pockets and low transaction costs push manipulative trades back. Insiders can only choose whether to reveal or hide private information.
- Racetrack market odds outperform predictions of racetrack experts (Figlewski 1979). Those who do best at betting on horse races "are smart in ways they can not articulate, and in ways unrelated to I.Q." (Ceci & Liker, 1986).
- No quantitative estimates of adverse selection costs, informed trader extraction rates, or market maker worst-case loss.

**Verdict:** Discusses informed vs. uninformed dynamics qualitatively. Informed market makers dominate price discovery. No quantitative insider extraction estimates.

## PM167 — Snowberg, Wolfers & Zitzewitz (2013) "Prediction Markets for Economic Forecasting"

**Focus:** Survey/handbook chapter on prediction market design, efficiency, accuracy vs. polls/forecasters. NBER Working Paper 18222.

**Relevant data (insider/informed trading):**
- **Insider information as market failure:** "Noise traders may quite rationally choose not to trade in markets where there is a high degree of insider information." Example: Supreme Court nominee markets routinely fail because traders know very few people hold actual information. This is the adverse selection death spiral — uninformed withdraw when informed concentration is too high.
- **Prohibition of insider trading necessary for function:** "a market to predict the ISM's business confidence measure would be unlikely to function if it were well known that ISM employees were trading in it." The mere perception of insider presence destroys liquidity.
- **Favorite-longshot bias quantified (Figure 3):** Rate of return per dollar bet ranges from near break-even for heavy favorites to -80% for extreme longshots (100/1+ odds). Data pooled across US 1992-2001, Australia 1991-2004, UK 1994-2004, plus historical studies (Griffith 1949, Weitzman 1965, Ali 1977, Jullien & Salanie 2000). Attributed to either asymmetric risk preferences or misperceptions of probabilities (Snowberg & Wolfers 2010).
- **Manipulation largely fails:** Rhode & Strumpf (2008) placed random $500 bets on IEM — prices quickly returned to pre-manipulation levels. Camerer (1998) placed large bets in parimutuel horse racing (cancelled before race) — no bandwagon effect. Lab experiments: manipulators had "little evidence" of success at distorting prices. One exception: a single large trader on Intrade kept Hillary Clinton electability prices elevated for a significant period, but suffered "huge losses."
- **Macro derivatives vs. forecasters (Table 2):** Prediction markets reduced forecast errors by ~5.5% relative to surveys of ~30 professional forecasters (Goldman Sachs/Deutsche Bank Economic Derivatives, Oct 2002-Jul 2005). In Fair-Shiller regressions, market-based forecast coefficient indistinguishable from 1; survey coefficient insignificantly different from zero in most series.
- **Corporate insider bias:** Google internal prediction markets showed "optimistic bias" — outcomes favorable to Google over-predicted. More pronounced among newer employees and on days Google stock appreciated.
- No direct quantitative estimates of informed trader extraction rates or adverse selection costs per trade.

**Verdict:** Key finding is that insider information concentration kills prediction markets (adverse selection). The favorite-longshot bias is the primary documented pricing anomaly — longshot bettors lose up to 80% of stake. No extraction rate estimates.

## PM168 — Erikson & Wlezien (2008) "Are Political Markets Really Superior to Polls as Election Predictors?"

**Focus:** Challenges claim that IEM election markets outperform polls. Analyzes IEM presidential markets 1988-2004.

**Relevant data (insider/informed trading):**
- **Market inefficiency quantified:** A trader using properly discounted poll projections would have picked the undervalued candidate in the winner-take-all market 87% of the time, reaping a 15% profit on investment. In the vote-share market, the profit rate from poll-based trading would be ~1.4% per trade — modest but statistically near-certain (p < 0.0001).
- **Markets overestimate uncertainty:** Winner-take-all prices put odds at ~50-50 regardless of poll certainty when >91 days from election. Markets assign "a wide variance to the expected vote" — effectively overvaluing longshots. This is the political market version of the favorite-longshot bias.
- **Market prices vs. poll projections (Table 2):** Mean absolute error of vote-share market prices = 2.41 percentage points vs. 2.13 for poll-based projections. In 55% of cases, projected polls were closer to actual outcome. With weekly averaging, polls beat markets 63% of the time with mean error of 1.7 points (30% improvement over market).
- **Where markets fail:** Early in campaigns (>90 days out), market prices respond to information that is "often quite wrong." Markets only catch up to polls within 30 days of election. "Where the market holds to a view of the election at odds with the poll projections, it is somewhat more likely to be wrong than right."
- **Information content test:** Following market deviations from poll projections leads to correct direction only 52% of the time (vs. 50% chance). Markets contain marginal positive information beyond polls, but mostly noise.
- No data on insider/informed trader extraction rates, adverse selection costs, or market maker losses.

**Verdict:** Documents market inefficiency exploitable by informed (poll-literate) traders — 15% return in winner-take-all markets. No insider extraction data.

## PM172 — Chiappori, Salanie, Salanie & Gandhi (2008) "From Aggregate Betting Data to Individual Risk Preferences"

**Focus:** Identifies distribution of individual risk preferences from aggregate parimutuel horse race betting data (25,000+ races, 200,000+ horses, US 2001). Tests expected utility vs. non-expected utility models.

**Relevant data (informed trading/insider dynamics):**
- **Favorite-longshot bias confirmed empirically:** Bettors choose longshots too frequently. Favorites offer better expected returns. The bias is explained not by risk-aversion but by nonlinear probability weighting — bettors distort small probabilities upward.
- **Expected return by odds:** From raw data, expected return p(R+1) - 1 is most negative for extreme longshots (highest odds) and approaches zero or slightly negative for favorites (lowest odds). The nonparametric curve shows the classic pattern: favorites yield ~-15% to -18% return, longshots yield -30% to -80% depending on odds.
- **Parimutuel take rate:** Track take t heavily concentrated around 18% (10th percentile = 16.5%, 90th percentile = 20.9%). This is the baseline "house edge" before any informed/uninformed dynamics.
- **Risk preference heterogeneity:** Relative risk aversion varies enormously across bettors (coefficient ranges from 2 to 25+ in survey literature). In their model, a single dimension of heterogeneity (taste for risk) determines self-selection: lower-theta bettors bet favorites, higher-theta bet longshots.
- **Normalized Fear of Ruin (NF) index:** For the representative agent, NF crosses 1 (risk-neutral threshold) — agent is risk-loving for small and large odds, slightly risk-averse for moderate odds (~5-10). CRRA and CARA parametric specifications strongly rejected by the data. EU models fit poorly; non-expected utility (especially rank-dependent EU with probability weighting) dominates.
- **Key structural finding:** Probability weighting (Yaari/RDEU) explains the favorite-longshot bias better than risk preferences alone. The "overweighting of small probabilities" drives longshot over-betting. This means uninformed bettors systematically transfer value to those who bet favorites — a structural informed-vs-uninformed extraction mechanism.
- **Model comparison (Table 3, BIC):** Risk-neutral baseline: -92124.3. Best homogeneous NEU: +73.1 improvement. EU heterogeneous: +56.7. Heterogeneity adds little beyond probability weighting — the bias is primarily cognitive, not preference-driven.
- No direct quantification of informed trader extraction rates or adverse selection costs. The paper is about risk preferences, not strategic interaction between informed and uninformed.

**Verdict:** Quantifies the structural mechanism (probability weighting) that creates systematic value transfer from longshot bettors to favorite bettors. The 18% take rate is the house extraction. No insider-specific data.
